# ControlQuest — Research integration and implementation brief

Prepared 19 November 2026. Repository: https://github.com/pm470/ControlQuest/tree/gui ; reviewed source snapshot `a7ab364` (lowercase `gui`). This is a proposal, not an implemented change or a claim that simulations were rerun. All calibration outcomes below are existing repository expectations; verify them in the course Docker image before reporting new numerical results.

## 1. Scope and scientific contribution

ControlQuest is an interactive case-study laboratory for understanding design trade-offs in model predictive control (MPC). A common 2D point-mass benchmark exposes how objective weights, the horizon/terminal set, nonconvex geometry, model mismatch and disturbances/update frequency affect closed-loop outcomes. The player serves as controller designer, proposes changes and observes actual acados results. No new sophisticated research algorithm is required. The goal is a pedagogical demonstration of research motivations and trade-offs, not a claim to reproduce or outperform the cited methods.

Keep the six current experiences (tutorial plus five real levels), the existing aesthetic and scoring, and the same short course-project scope. Deliver a reusable research debrief, two recent publications per level, one small meaningful experiment/plot per scored level, and a reproducible benchmark summary. Extra controllers and an automated planner are stretch goals only.

### Architecture and navigation

Current: level select → intro (`LevelIntroWidget`) → gameplay (`LevelPlayWidget`) → `ResultPanel` overlay. `LevelScreen` currently manages intro/play via `_intro`, `_play`, `_other` and `_on_play`; it preserves the play widget while viewing intro. The tutorial subclasses the gameplay widget and has additional next-level buttons. `LevelInfo` is the metadata dataclass. The host-side GUI uses PySide6; the Docker engine runs acados and CasADi; `RunResult` / `Trajectory` already contain results; do not modify the solver or protocol for static content. `point-mass-mpc/` is the previous sandbox, not the game.

Proposed: level intro (existing; at most a one-line research question) → gameplay/retry (existing) → result overlay (existing) → optional **Research debrief** within the same LevelScreen → next level. Give players immediate Retry and Replay on every failure, an optional `Research insights` action after any valid result, and make `View debrief` the recommended route after a successful first completion. Preserve an explicit direct Next/Skip action for unlocked next levels. On final level offer `Back to levels`/`Finish`. Keep access available after failure and after completing the level; no research reading requirement for unlocking stars. A player who goes back should retain knobs, replay position and experiment history; avoid restarting the solver. Retain existing tutorial no-stars/unlocking behavior. Proposed optional level-card research-only access if needed, with clearly labelled no-run state.

Debrief design: concise headline/one-sentence takeaway; `Your experiment` with actual completed-run metrics and one small plot; `Why it matters` (real-world application); two compact paper cards (title, authors/year/venue/DOI, problem, solution, connection to the game; links opened externally only by user click); a clearly labelled `What the game simplifies` note. One primary CTA, level badge/accent, borderless cards/lips, theme tokens, existing `math_text()` formatting and scrollable responsive content (900×560 minimum). Avoid a wall of paper abstracts, compulsory quizzes or a plot dashboard. Static paper content available offline; opening papers is optional. Cite paraphrases, no copied figures without explicit license/attribution.

## 2. Level-by-level research content and experiments

Publications selected from 2022–2025 (within the requested five-year window on the review date). The selections are peer-reviewed journal or conference papers, with DOI and/or author/conference full-text links. Short summaries are paraphrases based on the publications/author records; some publisher full texts were inaccessible, so avoid asserting exact quantitative results unless checked in the paper. ### Level 0 — Hello, MPC (`hello_mpc`)

Research question: How does a finite-horizon open-loop optimization become a feedback controller, and why does doing it repeatedly require efficient numerical algorithms?

Game: guided measure → predict/optimize → apply first input → repeat tour. Point mass, dt=0.1 s, N=30 (3 s), fixed weights; no stars. Model is a discrete double integrator, not a neural model. In `closed_loop.simulate`, the solver is called each sample, then the first input is applied. In the GUI the trajectory is computed before replay; replay timing is not evidence of real-time execution.

Papers:

- [0A] J. Frey, K. Baumgärtner, G. Frison, M. Diehl (2025), “Multi-Phase Optimal Control Problems for Efficient Nonlinear Model Predictive Control With acados,” *Optimal Control Applications and Methods*, 46, 827–845. DOI https://doi.org/10.1002/oca.3234 ; full text https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07382. Problem: accurate horizon formulations can be costly. Solution: acados multi-phase interface with different discretizations, models and control parameterizations over the horizon. Connection: ControlQuest uses the same solver framework to solve a much simpler, single-phase OCP at every step. Read Introduction and Sec. 3.1 for the relation to receding horizon; Sec. 5 for performance comparison.
- [0B] M. Krinner, A. Romero, L. Bauersfeld, M. Zeilinger, A. Carron, D. Scaramuzza (2024), “MPCC++: Model Predictive Contouring Control for Time-Optimal Flight with Safety Constraints,” *Robotics: Science and Systems*. https://roboticsconference.org/2024/program/papers/109/ ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17551. Problem: fast drone racing needs prediction, safety and accurate models. Solution: track/terminal constraints, learned residual dynamics and Bayesian parameter tuning. Connection: a demanding real application of repeated predictive optimization, with multiple design decisions that later game levels explore in isolation. Read abstract, opening figures and problem formulation.

UI takeaway: “One optimized plan is not the controller. The policy emerges by solving again from the measured state.” Show two consecutive plans/timestamps and highlight the first applied control. Label 3 s horizon vs 0.1 s update; show actual solve count and mean solver time from result if available. No extra experiment required for tutorial.

### Level 1 — The Price of Speed (`price_of_speed`)

Research question: How do MPC weights balance competing closed-loop objectives, and why is controller tuning a multi-objective optimization problem in its own right?

Game: q_p, q_v, r adjust the position/velocity state weights and input penalty; terminal weight P=10Q. The input budget is cumulative `E_u = sum(dt * ||u||²)`; default expected battery_empty, reference q_p=0.1, q_v=1, r=1 → 3 stars. Budget is 5; time limit 40s, 2-star threshold 30s, 3-star effort threshold 4.75. E_u is a quadratic actuation-effort proxy, not measured physical energy in joules. It is an external termination/scoring rule, not a hard cumulative-energy OCP constraint. Weight ratios determine the exact optimum if every objective term is multiplied by a positive scalar; solver numerical tolerances and unscaled soft penalties can affect equivalence in general. Do not label the default as literally time-optimal or any reference as globally Pareto optimal.

Papers:

- [1A] D. Stenger, R. Ritschel, R. Voßwinkel, H. Richter (2023), “What Is the Best Way to Optimally Parameterize the MPC Cost Function for Vehicle Guidance?” *Mathematics*, 11(2), 465. DOI https://doi.org/10.3390/math11020465. Problem: tuning many weights for vehicle tracking and comfort with conflicting objectives and possibly failed simulations. Solution: compare multiobjective Bayesian optimization, particle swarm and NSGA-II methods, treating the closed-loop simulation as a black-box evaluation. Connection: each player run evaluates a proposed weight vector much like a black-box tuner; tracking speed and effort compete. Read introduction and Pareto-front illustration; do not implement BO for this course project.
- [1B] M. Abtahi, M. Rabbani, S. Nazari (2023), “An Automatic Tuning MPC with Application to Ecological Cruise Control,” *IFAC-PapersOnLine*, 56(3), 265–270. DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ifacol.2023.12.035 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09358. Problem: fuel-saving cruise-control performance depends on cost-function weights and upcoming road geometry. Solution: offline dynamic programming, inverse optimization and a learned weight predictor. Connection: adjusting trade-off weights affects actual closed-loop effort; the game manual tuning is a simple analog, not a fuel-model reproduction. Read the example objective and introduction.

Optional more directly energy-aware paper: N. Zhang et al. (2024), “Bayesian Optimization for Automatic Tuning of an Energy Aware 2-D Vehicle Platoon,” *IEEE Transactions on Intelligent Vehicles*, 9(11), 7380–7391. https://doi.org/10.1109/TIV.2024.3397812. Authors' code: https://github.com/ZNianHua/BOMPC.

UI: completed-run scatter time-to-target (s) vs effort proxy, highlight current, failed/partial runs distinctly and exclude failures from the success Pareto set. Compute nondominated points only among completed successful runs; label “empirical nondominated set of tested settings,” not true Pareto front. Use actual run snapshots (`result.knobs`, `metrics.time`, `metrics.energy`, `outcome`). On failure show effort and distance remaining, never treat failure time as travel time. Optional predefined calibration sweep with `python calibrate.py price_of_speed --sweep`; no GUI sweep needed. A player who has tried only one configuration sees their point and a prompt to compare another, not a fabricated frontier.

### Level 2 — Brake Check (`brake_check`)

Research question: How can a feasible OCP now lead to an infeasible OCP later, and how do terminal constraints preserve future feasibility?

Game: target x=48 m, wall x=50 m, max acceleration per axis 1 m/s², max x-speed 4 m/s. Default N=10 (1s) without terminal stop expects controller_failure; reference N=20 with terminal stop expects 3 stars; N=30 without stop expects 2, N=5 with stop timeout. Along x, full-speed stopping time 4 s and stopping distance 8 m. With terminal stop v_N=0, the feasible terminal set consists of admissible resting states; apply zero input to hold state. In the exact deterministic double-integrator with hard wall/input bounds, initial feasible plan, no disturbances and valid exact constraint-solving, the shifted prior plan plus zero input supplies a feasible candidate at the next sample. This proves the nominal recursive-feasibility mechanism, not general closed-loop asymptotic stability/real-hardware safety. Short N bounds speed by approximately a_max*N*dt for the one-dimensional motion. N is always known to solver; a wall is not literally unseen, but its consequence lies outside the finite planned interval. Distinguish solver/invalid-plan failure from certified mathematical infeasibility; in this level compare stopping distance and wall distance for physical evidence.

Papers:

- [2A] M. E. Geurts, A. Katriniok, E. Silvas, N. J. Brouwer, W. P. M. H. Heemels (2025), “Model Predictive Control for Lane Merging Automation With Recursive Feasibility Guarantees and Its Experimental Validation,” *IEEE Transactions on Control Systems Technology*, 33(2), 566–581. https://doi.org/10.1109/TCST.2024.3485306 ; author university record https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/model-predictive-control-for-lane-merging-automation-with-recursi-3/ . Problem: safe automated lane merging and continuing with cruise control. Solution: terminal sets designed for recursive feasibility under safety/velocity constraints; validation in simulations and vehicle experiments. Connection: terminal sets ensure there is a safe continuation, just as rest is an invariant terminal condition in the game. Shorter open 2023 conference version: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ifacol.2023.10.1255 (same research lineage, not counted as separate paper), https://pure.tue.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/313571025/1-s2.0-S2405896323016592-main.pdf.
- [2B] Z. Zhao, A. Girard, S. Olaru (2024), “Nonlinear model predictive control based on K-step control invariant sets,” *European Journal of Control*, 80, 101040. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejcon.2024.101040. Problem: standard control invariant terminal sets can be hard to compute for nonlinear systems. Solution: generalize them to K-step invariant sets, constructed using symbolic control, for recursive feasibility. Connection: terminal stop is an especially easy invariant terminal set; real systems require richer constructions. Focus on abstract and introductory discussion, not symbolic-control implementation.

UI: one plot of x-direction stopping distance v_x²/(2*a_max) vs wall distance (for x-positive motion); mark first crossing, if it exists; optionally use existing `point_of_no_return` metrics and brake gauge for consistency. Compare N, terminal stop, outcome, actual mean solve latency, travel time only for success. Do not assume N is measured computation time: it is OCP size proxy. Provide “Shift plan, append zero input” three-step graphic or brief derivation.

### Level 3 — Dead End (`dead_end`)

Research question: Why can local nonlinear optimization fail to find a collision-free route despite a route existing, and is longer horizon alone enough to escape a trap?

Game: U-shaped obstacle, inflated ellipses with nonconvex exterior constraints (implemented via soft slack penalties, also validated against allowed entry). SQP warm-started from previous plan. Default and longer horizons N=30,60,100,150 expected to timeout; waypoint strategy changes the reference and is expected to succeed with a suitable waypoint. Waypoints are not optimizer initial guesses and are not global optimization; they guide local tracking using a new reference. A timeout is evidence of stagnating closed-loop behaviour in this map, not formal proof of a local optimum or guaranteed failure with longer N for all problems. Existing comparison widget already records experiments. AGENTS.md documents a trial of Dijkstra cost-to-go and RBF terminal fit (poor performance); a Dijkstra look-ahead reference is a promising alternative but unimplemented and explicitly awaiting team decision.

Papers:

- [3A] S.-M. Lee (2025), “Global-Initialization-Based Model Predictive Control for Mobile Robots Navigating Nonconvex Obstacle Environments,” *Actuators*, 14(9), 454. https://doi.org/10.3390/act14090454. Problem: a conventional local MPC solver can stay in a concave obstacle trap. Solution: selectively use a particle-swarm global search to supply the optimizer's initial trajectory guess. ROS/Gazebo simulations compare it with conventional IPOPT MPC. Connection: the exact same failure class as Dead End; ControlQuest fixes the reference instead of running global-search initialization. Read Sec. 2 and simulation comparison (Fig. 5).
- [3B] K. S. Narkhede, A. M. Kulkarni, D. A. Thanki, I. Poulakakis (2022), “A Sequential MPC Approach to Reactive Planning for Bipedal Robots Using Safe Corridors in Highly Cluttered Environments,” *IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters*. https://doi.org/10.1109/LRA.2022.3204367 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00156 . Problem: navigating clutter with a local controller while considering robot dynamics and obstacles. Solution: directed safe corridors, waypoint guidance, sequential MPC. Connection: giving local MPC intermediate goals is directly analogous to player-specified waypoints. Read corridor figure and simulation section.

Optional deeper: R. Soloperto, A. Mesbah, F. Allgöwer (2024 journal issue; DOI 2023), “Safe Exploration and Escape Local Minima With Model Predictive Control Under Partially Unknown Constraints,” *IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control*. https://doi.org/10.1109/TAC.2023.3283731 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.03614. Different partially unknown setting, richer exploration with backup trajectory.

UI: overlay trajectories for no fix, longer horizon, waypoint fix (if tried); show remaining target distance after timeout and successful travel time/mean solve latency. Keep existing “Your experiments,” reuse rather than duplicate history. Optional small Dijkstra-path advisory experiment (not needed for MVP; only after explicit team approval); if implemented call it global-reference guidance, not terminal cost unless it really changes V_f. Use actual solver ellipses for path feasibility not visual blocks alone.

### Level 4 — Heavy Ball (`heavy_ball`)

Research question: How do model errors propagate through predicted motion, and what is the difference between compensating for uncertainty and identifying a better model?

Game: model gain g=1 by default, actual g_true=0.6. Plant acceleration is g_true*u instead of g*u. The reference g=0.6, margin=0.1 expects 3 stars. Test drive starts from rest with a=1 m/s², T=2s: p_model=0.5*g*a*T², predicted 2m for g=1 and measured 1.2m (derived from configured test drive; verify returned measurement). Estimate g=2*p_measured/(a*T²). A 10-step-old plan vs current actual position displays the deviation. This is manual offline parameter identification followed by certainty-equivalent MPC; not automatic adaptive MPC, not physical mass estimation, not a formal uncertainty bound. The level rule explicitly says margin alone does not prevent the calibrated failure; too much margin closes the bay; intro currently implies margins solve it. Correct that discrepancy. A geometric buffer is a heuristic—not a verified robust tube. A multi-step old-plan deviation also contains effects of intervening replans, so it is a realized plan deviation, not pure open-loop model-identification error. Use the independent constant-input test-drive for direct model error.

Papers:

- [4A] A. Sasfi, M. N. Zeilinger, J. Köhler (2023), “Robust adaptive MPC using control contraction metrics,” *Automatica*, 155, 111169. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.automatica.2023.111169 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11713 (accepted manuscript). Problem: parametric mismatch and additive disturbance can make nominal predictions unsafe. Solution: uncertainty tubes, set-membership estimation and model updates to reduce conservatism, with robust guarantees under assumptions; planar quadrotor numerical example. Connection: game juxtaposes clearance buffer and better parameter; rigorous robust/adaptive MPC combines them more carefully. Read intro and example.
- [4B] B. Xu, A. Suleman, Y. Shi (2023), “A multi-rate hierarchical fault-tolerant adaptive model predictive control framework: Theory and design for quadrotors,” *Automatica*, 153, 111015. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.automatica.2023.111015. Problem: unexpected actuator faults affect requested-vs-actual thrust. Solution: adaptive parameter estimator integrated with Lyapunov-based MPC in translation loop, separate rotation control and dual sample rates; achieves fault-tolerant tracking in numerical simulations. Connection: Heavy Ball represents loss of actuation effectiveness as a scalar gain; player identifies it with a test rather than online estimator. Read abstract, problem/motivation and simulation plots if available.

UI: model vs observed test-drive distance, estimated gain and player-selected gain (from test drive response and run snapshot, do not leak hidden value before test or reveal solution in intro). Plot realized predicted-vs-actual position deviation over time (`trajectory.prediction_error`, initial 10 undefined samples excluded). Compare model correction vs changing margin while holding other variable constant. Include outcome, sampled min block clearance, max realized deviation and time for success. No need to implement estimator or tube MPC.

### Level 5 — Crosswind (`crosswind`)

Research question: How frequently must one re-optimize to retain useful feedback under disturbances while respecting a limited compute budget?

Game: model ignores additive wind; plant sees deterministic seeded gusts. N=30, dt=0.1s, periodic k in {1,2,3,5,10,20}, replan interval k*dt; if k>1 the controller executes multiple previously optimized inputs open-loop between solves. Default k=1,m=0.1 expected solve_budget; reference k=10,m=0.3 expected 3 stars. 60 solve budget, 35 solves for 3 stars. Solve count is a resource proxy, not actual CPU-time budget; actual mean/total solver time may be separately reported. Fixed-period multi-step MPC is NOT event-triggered or self-triggered MPC; those are research extensions. k=1 does not guarantee safety/perfect disturbance rejection. Drift is measured relative to executing plan; it resets to near zero when solve occurs. Check max drift immediately before replanning separately if desired; sampled min clearance is not a continuous-time distance certificate.

Papers:

- [5A] P. Li, T. Wang, Y. Kang, K. Li, Y.-B. Zhao (2022), “Event-based model predictive control for nonlinear systems with dynamic disturbance,” *Automatica*, 145, 110533. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.automatica.2022.110533 ; publisher PDF mirrored by authors https://www.niclab.ac.cn/assets/pdf/2022-Event-Based%20Model%20Predictive%20Control%20for%20Nonlinear%20Systems%20with%20Dynamic%20Disturbance.pdf . Problem: frequent OCP solves consume resources yet unknown disturbances corrupt plans. Solution: event- and self-triggered disturbance-prediction MPC, constraint tightening and prediction-error-based schedules. Connection: player selects fixed k; paper intelligently varies the next update and considers disturbances in its prediction, a more advanced version of the same trade-off. Read intro and simulation comparison.
- [5B] P. Conrad, K. Graichen (2024), “A sensitivity-based approach to self-triggered nonlinear model predictive control,” *IEEE Access*, 12, 153243–153252. https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2024.3480522 ; author record https://cris.fau.de/publications/331855152/ . Problem: fixed high-frequency MPC is resource intensive. Solution: choose next sampling time accounting for sensitivity of the MPC cost and a cost-decrease condition; sensitive situations get more frequent updates. Connection: real controllers can adapt k to current conditions rather than applying one fixed value everywhere; game illustrates why. Read abstract and numerical examples.

UI: plot drift versus time with solve markers from `trajectory.solve_steps`; annotate open-loop runs, solve count, margin and outcome. Compare solve counts vs max sampled drift with fixed margin and the same seed. Budget failures are truncated; do not compare short failed runs as if they completed. An optional advanced experiment could trigger a replan when prediction deviation exceeds a threshold, but do not implement unless approved; a heuristic threshold is not a proof of recursive feasibility/stability and would alter protocol/budget/calibration.

Deliverables: shared `docs/RESEARCH.md` with core research question and reproducible benchmark table, references, limitations, and links; optionally generated CSV/JSON/figures only after actual runs.
